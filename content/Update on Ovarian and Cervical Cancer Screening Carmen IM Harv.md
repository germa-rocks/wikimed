### 🖼️ SLIDE 6: Limitations and Challenges of Screening
*   **Potential benefits:**
    *   Reduce ovarian cancer mortality
*   Ovarian cancer mortality directly related to stage at dx
*   Screening program would need to detect early-stage disease
*   Poor OS rate is likely related to disease spread at dx outside the ovary
*   **Early detection focusing only on ovaries likely to miss many tumors**
*   Model of unifocal disease beginning in ovaries and progressing to diffuse disease is plausible
*   Important to recognize that some ovarian cancers may be **multifocal and extra-ovarian**
    *   Originate in fallopian tube
    *   Carcinomatosis may develop after BSO (peritoneal primary)
---

**04:26**
So obviously, there are potential benefits to screening. If you can detect the cancer early, you'll decrease mortality. Obviously, you need a good screening program to allow for detection of early stage disease. The poor overall survival rate in ovarian cancer is now likely secondary to the inability to screen for early stage disease. And then a significant challenge remains in that recurrent cancer of the ovary is treatable but not curable. So we also are looking at ways to develop new therapeutic options for these patients when they develop recurrent disease. 

**05:06**
And then you know, one of the challenges that we have and many of you know this, is that it's been challenging in our field to establish a clear site of origin to ovarian cancer. At the Brigham and Women's Hospital within our system, a lot of investigations were done about 10-15 years ago trying to understand how many of these cancers begin in the ovary proper and how many of them actually begin in the fallopian tube and then secondarily spread to the ovary. Today we recognize that there are three different sites of origin for ovarian cancer, or what we call "ovarian cancer in quotations": one is the ovary proper, the second site may be the fallopian tube, and then third site of origin may be the peritoneum. As you know, when young women are developing embryologically, the coelomic epithelium gives rise to the cells that ultimately form the ovaries, the fallopian tubes, and the peritoneum. So these three structures carry the same genetic lineage and they therefore are susceptible to the same oncogenic changes.

### 🖼️ SLIDE 7: Potential Harms of Screening
*   **Risks of false-positive results**
    *   Surgical evaluation and associated risks of surgical procedures
    *   Psychological stress
*   Most experts opine that screening program should have **positive predictive value (PPV) of at least 10%**
    *   For each screen-detected ovarian cancer, no more than 9 women undergo unnecessary procedures for false-positive results
    *   A PPV of at least 10% requires specificity of at least 99.6%, assuming sensitivity of 80% for screening for all women > 50 years
---

**06:06**
The harms of screening... So if you have a good screening program, it can actually afford improvements in overall survival. You can detect early stages of cancer and make an intervention that is hopefully going to be less radical. But there are also risks in screening. The risk is primarily those of false positive results. In the particular case of ovarian cancer, you can end up actually taking somebody to the operating room, taking their ovaries out, and potentially causing harm that way either through the risk of the surgical procedure itself, or in women that are premenopausal or that are still benefiting from their ovarian function, putting them through surgical menopause or short-changing their exposure to endogenous estrogens even beyond menopause. 

**06:53**
So most experts will give the opinion that for a screening program to be successful, you need to have a tool that has a positive predictive value of at least 10%. That means that for each screen-detected ovarian cancer, there will be no more than 9 women who undergo unnecessary procedure for a false positive result. And if you keep this number in mind as we go through the trials that I'll offer in some detail, you'll see how most of these trials have failed to meet this goal of having a positive predictive value of at least 10%. Positive predictive value of 10% will require a specificity of at least 99.6% for a screening program in ovarian cancer early detection to be successful. And that assumes a sensitivity of 80% for screening for all women over the age of 50. Ovarian cancer, like many other cancers, becomes more prevalent as women get older, and the incidence begins to really creep up after the age of 50.

### 🖼️ SLIDES 11 & 12: Challenges & Screened Population Features
**Challenges**
*   Given the low prevalence of ovarian cancer (40 cases per year per 100,000 women), screening test must have both high sensitivity and high specificity.
*   Require: 
    *   Sensitivity at least 75% 
    *   Specificity of more than 99.6% 
    *   To achieve a positive predictive value of 10%

**Screened Population Features**
1.  Disease has a high enough **prevalence** to justify screening 
2.  Medical care available if screening test is positive 
3.  Patient is willing & able to undergo further evaluation 
---

**13:05**
So given that ovarian cancer has a low prevalence, again there are approximately 40 cases per year per 100,000 women. So again lifetime probability of getting it is about 1%. Screening tools to be effective have to be both highly sensitive and also very specific. It requires for us to be able to meet this charge, it would require a test or combination of testing that has a sensitivity of at least 75% with a specificity of more than 99.6%. And again, this notion of a predictive, a positive predictive value of at least 10%. 

**13:42**
So when it comes to screening a population of women with ovarian cancer, we need a disease that has a high enough prevalence, we need medical care that's available if screening test is positive, and we need obviously a patient that is willing and able to undergo further evaluation if the screening test is positive.

### 🖼️ SLIDE 13: Issues Screening for Ovarian Cancer
*   **Failure to identify a histologic precursor lesion or a molecular event that precedes malignant transformation.**
    *   Ovarian cancer may develop from multiple foci within the abdomen
*   **Find a test that can detect not only clinically apparent ovarian cancer but also early disease long before it causes symptoms.**
---

**14:02**
So in ovarian cancer, unlike breast cancer where we've been able to identify precursor lesions such as ductal carcinoma in situ, we have not been able to identify a precursor histologic lesion or a molecular event that precedes malignant transformation. The closest we've gotten to this is what's called a TIC, or an intraepithelial lesion in the fallopian tube. But again, this is a very unique finding in the fallopian tube and does not necessarily predict or translate to finding something relevant or similar to that in the ovary or even in every fallopian tube. We think that ovarian cancer may actually have, may develop from multiple foci within the abdomen, and it may not just be a single oncogenic trigger event. 

**14:48**
And then we need to find a test that can detect not only the clinically apparent cancer but also disease long before it causes symptoms. When patients develop symptoms from ovarian cancer, they tend to be symptoms of upper abdominal metastatic disease. So these patients come in typically bloated, they have ascites, or they have 30% of our patients will come in with some kind of metastasis to the small bowel or the colon. So they may have constipation, diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, early satiety, weight loss, cachexia, fatigue. And by the time that these patients present with these symptoms, typically they are almost diagnostic of either stage 3C or 4 cancer.

### 🖼️ SLIDE 21: CA 125
*   **Remains the single most sensitive and specific marker for ovarian cancer to date.**
*   CA-125 is elevated in ~1% of healthy women
*   CA-125 level fluctuates during menstrual cycle, increases with age, varies with ethnicity and smoking status
*   Ovarian Cancer Incidence in Postmenopausal Women: 1 in 2,000 women/year
    *   CA125 Specificity (> 30U/ml): 98%
    *   CA125 Sensitivity: 63%
    *   **Positive Predictive Value: 2%**
*   **Low incidence and moderate specificity result in unacceptably low positive predictive value.**
---

**19:48**
So it remains however the most sensitive and specific marker for ovarian cancer. As I said earlier, it's elevated in about 1% of the healthy population. The CA 125 level is a biologic marker so it's going to fluctuate, and because it is also expressed in normal tissue it can fluctuate based on the patient's menstrual cycle. It increases with time, it can be increased in patients for example who smoke. Patients that have endometriosis and fibroids of the uterus will have an elevated CA 125 level related to those two benign conditions, and again when you look at ovarian cancer in postmenopausal women, 1 in 2,000 women per year will have a CA 125 specificity of 98% (greater than 35 units per ml, 30 units per ml of 98%). Sensitivity is pretty low at 63% and the positive predictive value, again where we want to get to a place of 10% or better, is only 2%. So the low incidence and the moderate specificity results become unacceptably low to actually allow us to think of it as a tool that has a positive predictive value that is relevant for the purposes of screening using the blood test alone.

### 🖼️ SLIDE 41: Summary: Limitations of Current Screening Strategies
*   **Low Specificity:** Most masses found by transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS) are benign (non-cancerous), leading to high false-positive rates.
*   **Unreliable Markers:** The CA-125 blood test is often elevated by common conditions like endometriosis, fibroids, or even normal menstruation, which can result in "false alarms".
*   **Aggressive Biology:** Many aggressive ovarian cancers spread very quickly, often before they reach a size detectable by current imaging.
