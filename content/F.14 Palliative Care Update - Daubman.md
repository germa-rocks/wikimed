### 📊 SLIDE: SUBSPECIALTY OF PALLIATIVE CARE
Palliative care is **specialized medical care** for people with **serious illnesses**. This type of care is focused on providing patients with **relief from the symptoms, pain, and stress** of a serious illness—whatever the diagnosis or prognosis. It is an **extra layer of support** to the patient’s other clinicians.
*Source: https://www.capc.org/about/palliative-care/*
---

I think language really matters here, and so the Center to Advance Palliative Care (CAPC) did some market research where they asked patients and families, "What is palliative care?" Most people didn't know. They asked physicians; most people thought it was synonymous with end-of-life care. But they tested out different definitions and ways to describe palliative care, and this is what they found to be the most palatable and the most comprehensive. 

So, when I walk into a room, or when I'm being referred to a case, I describe palliative care as **specialized medical care**. And that's intentional. Some people think palliative care is just like a nice person holding your hand while you die, and so I like to emphasize that it's a medical subspecialty.

And then I talk about it as care for people with **serious illness**. And that is intentional, that I don't say terminal illness, or end-stage illness, or for people at end of life. A) because that's not always true, and B) that terminology—serious illness—is found to be more acceptable to patients and families.

---
### 📊 SLIDE: WHAT PALLIATIVE CARE IS...
**What palliative care is...**
* Excellent, evidence-based medical treatment
* Vigorous care of pain and symptoms throughout illness
* Care that patients want *at the same time* as efforts to cure or prolong life

**What palliative care is not...**
* Not “giving up” on a patient
* Not in place of curative or life-prolonging care
* Not the same as hospice
---

And then I talk about all the things I can do. Oftentimes, when palliative care is described, with the best of intentions, we talk about, "Oh, there's nothing left to offer, here's palliative care," or "Here's all the things that we're taking away from you, and here's palliative care." So I like to focus on all the things that palliative care *does* provide. And so I say my goal is to really focus on **intensive symptom management**: the pain, the nausea, the anxiety, the stress that can go along with having a serious illness. And it's appropriate at any diagnosis and any prognosis. And so I always emphasize that it's for beyond just cancer diagnoses, or beyond just the end stage.

And then the last piece I think is important to mention is that it's an **extra layer of support** for the patient's clinicians as well. That I've had some patients say, "Well, I don't think I need you," and then I've had a really gracious oncologist say, "Of course, you're doing so well, Susan, that's wonderful. *I* feel like *I* need Dr. Daubman, because I want to make sure I'm really providing you with comprehensive care and I'm looking at your symptoms." And so saying that it's an extra layer of support for the patients, for the families, and the clinicians themselves.

And I think it's also important to show the partnership as well. That there's often this worry that palliative care is coming in because the primary care or the other specialists are going to sort of slowly back away, that there's nothing left to offer and we're going to abandon you while palliative care comes in. So with this language, we emphasize that it's a partnership: "I'm working *with* your cardiologist, with your primary care doctor, with your oncologist, to be an extra layer of support on top of the already incredible care that you're receiving." 

So it's a couple sentences, it's a little bit of a mouthful, but I think language really matters here, and the way that you introduce palliative care can really impact how open patients and families are to talking about palliative care.

I think the language is so important because there is a lot of confusion about what palliative care is, and it's evolved over time. It's a pretty new medical subspecialty.

### 📊 SLIDE: TRADITIONAL MODEL OF CARE AT THE END OF LIFE
*(Gráfico mostrando o modelo tradicional onde há uma divisão abrupta)*
* **Diagnosis -> Active Therapy (Life Prolonging Care)**
* *[Linha preta divisória]*
* **Dying -> Bereavement (PC / Hospice)**
---

So this is sort of the old-school model here: that you're diagnosed with some type of challenging serious illness, you get life-prolonging care, everything's going well, you're getting surgery, you're getting treatment, you're getting intervention, and then all of a sudden you sort of slam into that black line and... oops, there's nothing more we can do, and then palliative care comes in. And it's sort of synonymous with hospice, that palliative care is mentioned *as* the patient is dying. And it feels like a really clear demarcation, I think, to patients and families as well. 

That's sort of the old-school model. I tip my hand that I don't think that's the ideal model. What's found to be much more evidence-based and much more tolerated is when palliative care is involved as needed. 

---
### 📊 SLIDE: PALLIATIVE CARE FOR QUALITY OF LIFE “UPSTREAM”
*(Gráfico mostrando a integração progressiva)*
* **Disease Modifying Therapy** diminui gradualmente.
* **Palliative Care** aumenta gradualmente desde o diagnóstico.
* **Hospice** entra na fase de "Dying".
* Acompanhamento na fase de "Bereavement".
---

So there may be a time at diagnosis where it would be important to do some advance care planning and just think about the future, even if it's in a theoretical way. And so having palliative care involved either based on referral to the subspecialty or primary palliative care, or having the patient's own clinicians beginning having some of those conversations. And there may be a time, patients getting radiation therapy, is having a lot of symptoms, I dip back in, it would be really important to have intensive symptom management, symptoms are controlled, and then I dip back out. And so I think having palliative care evolve with the patient's needs feels much more important, and then you'll see there isn't that clear line that patients have to slam into, that it's having that conversation of "It's time for palliative care" doesn't have to be synonymous with "because you're not eligible for a transplant" or "because there's no more chemo we can offer for you." It can really be part of the natural disease trajectory. So that's sort of the goal of integrating palliative care upstream.

### 📊 SLIDE: HOSPICE VS. PALLIATIVE CARE
| | **Hospice** | **Palliative Care (includes Hospice)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Timing** | Less than 6-month prognosis if disease runs it’s expected course | Can occur at any stage of serious illness |
| **Treatment** | Focus is on comfort rather than aggressive disease-directed therapy | Care integrates disease-directed therapies with attention to quality of life and advance care planning |
| **Location** | Mainly provided in the home (relies upon the family caregiver and visiting hospice team). May also be provided in skilled nursing facility or hospice house | Often provided as outpatient or inpatient consultations |
---

And then I'm always clear with patients and families and referrers that palliative care *is* lots of things. It's not just the absence of things. That it's excellent, evidence-based medical treatment. It's vigorous—sometimes for patients who this would resonate with them, I say it's aggressive care. I'm providing intensive symptom management for you. It's not just taking things away. That it doesn't have to be in place of curative or life-prolonging care. It's not giving up, and it's not the same as hospice. 

Hospice is a very clear and clearly demarcated insurance benefit. And so it is true that hospice is for patients at end of life, that two physicians have to agree that *if* the disease runs its expected course, that the patient will die within six months. I will say, I think I had a patient on hospice for two and a half years, but every recertification period I just had to say, "No, I was wrong before, but I still do think that they have a prognosis of six months or less." So that's a much narrower benefit that we're able to offer, whereas palliative care can be at any stage. And then similarly, the focus really is on comfort for hospice, whereas palliative care can be much more open-minded about attention to quality of life and advance care planning *in a setting* that can happen where disease-directed therapies are occurring as well. 

And then palliative care generally can be provided as inpatient, outpatient, home-based, community consultations, whereas hospice is often a little bit more limited in that regard as well.

**If the patient, for example, was taking 60 mg of oxycodone in 24 hours:**
20 mg oxycodone / 30 mg oral morphine = 60 mg oxycodone / x mg oral morphine
x = 90 mg oral morphine/24 hours

### 📊 SLIDE: DISCUSSING PROGNOSIS CHANGES TREATMENT DECISIONS
