**Transcrição do Áudio (1:30 - 3:24):**
"And when I get called to the bedside to see a patient who's altered in the ICU, I really have a checklist that I go through. And the first thing I do is I try to do a **neurologic exam** on these patients and see if there's any focal findings or any evidence of a seizure. And if there is, this patient will be getting a head CT immediately. 

Um, if there's not, I'm gonna check a finger stick um to rule out things like hypoglycemia. I'll get an ABG rolling out hypercarbia or hypoxemia as the cause of the altered mental status. I'll check vital signs including blood pressure since both hypotension and hypertension can cause changes in sensorium. Uh, I'll check a temperature um since fever and hypothermia can both contribute to changes in mental status. And if I'm suspicious for infection uh I'll also check urine, uh chest x-ray, and blood cultures. Will I get a LP? Well it kind of depends, if they're having high fevers and a high white count I'll be more inclined to get um an LP as well, looking for a CSF infection.

And then I'm gonna review their medications. Is it something that we've done to them to cause them to be altered? Thinking about things like cefepime neurotoxicity or other medications. Um, I'll also check a tox screen for alcohol and drugs. Even if they're inpatient um it can still happen. So uh checking a tox screen is important. And then stat labs looking for things like uh hepatic encephalopathy, uh uremia, or hypo or hypernatremia that can change mental status. Getting an EKG might be important as well, thinking about patients who have um non-epileptiform status. 

And then finally I'm really thinking about, does my patient need a head CT? And the thing I'm trying to figure out is do they have some sort of acute finding, like an infarct, a hemorrhage, a mass, or hydrocephalus that is new and acute that would cause our altered mental status."

### [Slide 6: Checklist / Differential Diagnosis]
> **Checklist & Categorias:**
> 
> *   **Neurologic:** Infarct, Hemorrhage, Mass, Hydrocephalus, Seizures, Hypotension, HTN, Delirium
> *   **Toxic:** Medications, Alcohol, Drugs
> *   **Infectious:** UTI, PNA, Sepsis, Meningitis, Encephalitis
> *   **Metabolic:** Hypoglycemia, Hypercarbia, Hypoxemia, Fever, Hypothermia, HE, Uremia, Hyper/hyponatremia

> **Facts:**
> *   **94% of pts with AMS had a head CT**
> *   **Positive result in 11%** (ED > ICU > inpatient)
> 
> *(Ref: Acharya et al. Use of Computed Tomography of the Head in Patients With Acute Atraumatic Altered Mental Status: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis. JAMA Netw Open. 2022)*

**Transcrição do Áudio (4:00 - 5:02):**
"So this study looked at um folks who had a head CT in the intensive care unit and looked at the indication for the head CT and the likelihood of finding something abnormal. And as you can see, the **most common indication for a head CT was altered mental status (70%)**. With focal neurologic deficit and seizure-like activity coming in second and third, pretty far below altered mental status. However, **the incidence of an abnormal finding is lowest in this population** of patients with altered mental status, with only 7% having an abnormal CT finding. Focal neurologic deficit and seizures had an increased incidence of CT abnormalities. 

The study then looked at patient factors in the folks who had altered mental status to see if there was any trend, anything that would tell us who might be higher risk to have an abnormal CT finding. And they found **no patient factors** were associated with an acute change on head CT. You know, um, age, afib, anticoagulation, sedatives, uh malignancy didn't seem to make those patients more likely to have an abnormal CT finding."

**Transcrição do Áudio (5:02 - 5:58):**
"So when thinking about getting a head CT in your patients, you're really weighing risks and benefits. And the benefits of a head CT, well, it's rapid. It'll be done within minutes. It's accessible, most hospitals have CT scanners. The exam can be fairly limited in ICU patients. You get to rule out worst-case scenarios, and as you just saw, there's no clear risk factors for patients to have an abnormal head CT. 

However, the risks: the cost, the radiation to the patient, the resources—you're now pulling, you know, an ICU nurse away from the bedside to transport a patient down to CAT scan. It could cause a delay in the proper diagnosis. You might find something on the CT scan that's incidental, that isn't the cause of the altered mental status. And then the, um, the safety of transporting patients, especially critically ill patients, and the risks that involve."

> **Head CT in AMS - Risks vs. Benefits**

> *Riscos:* Cost, Radiation, Resources, Delay in diagnosis, Incidental findings, Transport.
> *Benefícios:* Rapid, Accessible, Exam limited in ICU pts, Rule out worst-case scenario, No clear risk factors for abml CT.

**Transcrição do Áudio (5:58 - 7:00):**
"So, thinking more about the critically ill population, this was a study, a retrospective observational study, looking at almost 2,500 head CTs in over 1,300 ICU patients. And they were looking for the incidence of abnormal head CTs in patients who had a head CT for altered mental status and looking for acute communicable findings such as hemorrhage, mass effect, infarction, and hydrocephalus. **Transcrição do Áudio (7:00 - 7:45):**
"So when I think about a head CT in a patient in the intensive care unit, I am really thinking about, does this patient have any red flags for an abnormal CAT scan? And I think about this **SAFE mnemonic**. 
So **S for seizures**, if they're having seizures, new seizures, I'm gonna want to get a CAT scan. 
If the change is **acute**, not a waxing and waning or slow decline, but an acute change in their sensorium, I'm going to get a head CT. 
If there's any **focal neurologic defect**. So it's important to go in and do a neuro exam, check pupils, check strength, see if um the patient can participate in a neurologic exam, check reflexes. 
And **E, was there an event?** Was there a fall or trauma or some sort of neurosurgical device that might make it more likely for them to have an abnormal head CT."

### [Slide 11: Red Flags (Mnemonic)]
> **Red Flags for Abnormal Head CT:**
> *   **S:** Seizures
> *   **A:** Acute change
> *   **F:** Focal neurological defect
> *   **E:** Event: fall/trauma/neurosurgical device

**Transcrição do Áudio (7:45 - 8:45):**
"So, when assessing for an altered mental status in the ICU, reassess the neuro exam. Consider that the patient's on sedation, paralytics. Check if there's any focal deficits. Rule out metabolic and toxic causes, think back to that first slide that had the four different categories of why people can have altered mental status and make sure you're thinking about all the causes and not just, you know, going for a head CT right away, but rather having a systematic approach to the patient who's altered. 

Screen for the SAFE red flags: seizures, acute findings, focal neurologic deficits, and an event. And if there are no red flags and metabolic, toxic uh etiology is likely, just observe the patient and recheck them. However, if they have a red flag or it's unclear, get a CT promptly. Because it's, while it's low, you know, 10% of patients in the MICU will have an abnormal um uh CAT scan."

### [Slide 12: Approach Flowchart]
> **Altered Mental Status**
> *   **Reassess neuro exam:** consider sedation, paralysis, focal defects
> *   **Rule out metabolic/toxic causes**
> *   **Screen for SAFE red flags**
> *   **If no red flags** and metabolic/toxic likely → **observe & recheck**
> *   **If ≥1 red flag or unclear → CT promptly**

**Transcrição do Áudio (8:45 - 9:24):**
"So key points here: Most delayed awakenings are not structural, but structural causes must be ruled out quickly when risk factors are present. About 1 in 10 patients with altered mental status will have an abnormal head CT. This is a significantly higher incidence, 1 in 4 patients in the ICU, especially neuro ICUs where it's particularly high. If there are any focal neurologic findings or new seizures, obtain a head CT. Use SAFE to remember the red flags and avoid knee-jerk 'just in case' CT. Balance the yield versus the transport risk. And document your thought process because it protects you."

### [Slide 13: Key Points Summary]
> **Key Points**
> *   Most delayed awakenings are not structural — *but structural causes must be ruled out quickly when risk factors are present.*
> *   About **1 in 10 patients** with altered mental status will have an abnormal head CT
> *   **Significantly higher incidence (1 in 4 patients) in the ICU**, especially neuro ICUs 
> *   If there are **focal neurologic findings or new seizures**, obtain a head CT
> *   Use **SAFE** to remember the red flags
> *   Avoid knee-jerk “just in case” CT — balance yield vs transport risk.
> *   Document your thought process — it protects patients and you.
